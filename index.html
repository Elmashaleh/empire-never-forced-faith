The Empire That Never Forced Faith

If Islam was spread by the sword, someone forgot to tell the millions who never converted.

Motaz Elmashaleh·The Hashemite University
Abstract

This paper examines two dimensions of Muslim rule in al-Andalus: the legal mechanisms that structured relations with conquered Christian and Jewish populations, and the scientific contributions later transmitted to Western Europe. The available record does not support the claim that Islamization in Iberia proceeded through systematic coercion — religious minorities kept legal protection and freedom of worship, and conversion unfolded gradually across more than three centuries.

Keywords — al-Andalus, dhimma, religious conversion, Islamic science, Toledo School of Translators

A force of seven thousand men crossed a strait, faced an empire, and within seven years controlled nearly an entire peninsula. For eight hundred years, that empire did not collapse, did not retreat, and did not need to force a single conversion to survive.

If Islam was spread by the sword, someone forgot to tell the millions of Christians and Jews who lived, worshipped, and prospered under Muslim rule for centuries without ever becoming Muslim.

The conventional narrative is simple and convenient: conquest by the sword, rule by coercion, faiths erased. The record kept across thirteen centuries tells a different story.

Islamization was not instant. It was gradual.

By the most rigorous quantitative estimates available, it took over three hundred years for Muslims to become a majority in al-Andalus — far too slow a timeline for any policy of coercion to explain.

The Conquest of 711

In the spring of 711, Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād's force met the Visigothic king Roderic at Guadalete. Roderic died on the field — and the kingdom that had ruled Iberia for three centuries went with him. What followed wasn't a massacre. City after city fell to terms, not the sword.

City after city did not fall to massacre — they fell to terms. Capitulation agreements were signed across the peninsula, preserving local governance in exchange for tribute rather than conversion.

The Dhimma System

Christians and Jews were classified as dhimmis — protected people — under a legal contract centuries older than al-Andalus itself. It ran in both directions. A ruler who broke it was breaking his own state's law, not just behaving unkindly.

The Conversion Curve

Richard Bulliet's quantitative study of conversion — built from biographical dictionaries and the dating of personal names — found the same pattern everywhere, including Iberia: a slow, S-shaped curve stretched across centuries, never a spike after conquest.

Two Libraries, One Gap

While Europe's largest monastic libraries held a few hundred volumes, the Caliphal Library of Córdoba under al-Hakam II is said to have held over 400,000.
